Balancing expected return and stochastic-dominance centrality: portfolio composition, decision stability and out-of-sample performance

Prospective research protocol · Version 1.0 · 25 November 2026 · Prepared for the Baltic portfolio project

P1 technical pilot completed; full nested study remains pending. Twenty-four pilot cases were independently checked on 25 November 2026. The restricted archive does not establish a global Pareto frontier. Section 3.6.1: model, methodology and results and interactive pilot results distinguish completed work from the prospective full experiment. The new 30% direct-US position rule applies to a separate new revision, not these sealed pilot results.

Abstract

This study will investigate whether jointly targeting expected return and net stochastic-dominance centrality (netSD) produces portfolios that generalise better than single-objective and existing portfolio models. Matched weighted-sum and ε-constraint grids, each with 100 settings, will produce attained Pareto approximations using information available at each decision date. The composition of the resulting portfolios will identify decisions that persist across preferences, estimation samples and optimisation starts. A nested chronological design will distinguish parameter selection from external evaluation. Six-month estimation windows, one-month tests and weekly forecast origins will match the existing study wherever its sealed inputs and model definitions permit a valid comparison. Performance, final wealth, turnover, liquidity, computational cost and the stability of asset-level decisions will be reported jointly. CPU and CUDA work will be scheduled under a shared resource budget and independently verified before publication.

1. Introduction

A portfolio that maximises a historical performance criterion may exploit sampling noise, while a portfolio that maximises expected return may concentrate in assets with uncertain forecasts. netSD evaluates a candidate relative to a reference network of return distributions; it is neither a direct monetary payoff nor a substitute for expected wealth. The proposed study makes the trade-off explicit and examines whether changing the preference for expected return changes a few marginal positions or overturns the entire allocation.

The principal contribution will be an empirical connection between the return–netSD trade-off, asset-level trading stability and genuinely subsequent performance under the project's execution constraints. No claim of a globally new optimisation method or guaranteed generalisation is made. The scientific question is whether preference-insensitive decisions also survive changes in data and market regime, and whether a feasible consensus policy performs competitively after costs.

The preregistered hypotheses are: H1, the joint model improves at least one primary OOS outcome without a material deterioration in the other relative to the netSD-only baseline; H2, cross-preference decision stability predicts lower turnover and smaller IS-to-OOS degradation; H3, apparent stability differs between trading permissions and market regimes. A negative result, an empty stable-buy core or indistinguishable Pareto points will be reported rather than hidden.

2. Related literature and conceptual positioning

The ε-constraint literature constructs multiobjective trade-offs by optimising one objective subject to bounds on the others. Mavrotas [1] develops an augmented implementation designed to avoid weakly efficient solutions. This motivates a return-threshold formulation here, but its exact-solution guarantees do not transfer automatically to a discontinuous, integer-constrained heuristic portfolio search. Both normalised weighted sums and ε-constraints are primary comparators; neither finite heuristic grid is treated as a certified complete frontier.

Repeated strategy search creates selection bias. Bailey et al. [2] discuss backtest overfitting and the importance of accounting for the number of tried configurations. Our protection is a chronological outer test, inner-only selection, registered grid sizes and paired inference. Stability selection [3] motivates examining how selections persist under data perturbation. Our preference-grid and time-series frequencies are descriptive stability measures: they do not inherit the original paper's false-discovery guarantees. Multiperiod portfolio research [4] also motivates explicit cash accounting, self-financing trades and transaction costs. These requirements remain essential even when a candidate has an attractive dominance score.

3. Methodology

3.1 Information sets, data and estimands

At origin t, every forecast, regime filter, scale parameter and selection decision must be measurable using the information available by t. Use genuine dated quotes, NAV publication timing, EUR conversion and the existing investable mask. Reference-only secondary-list shares may enter the dominance network but cannot be purchased. The first replication track retains the existing study's sealed dates, inventory, universe and execution conventions. A separate prospective track uses the latest confirmed holdings, including NVDA 3.277507; it must not be mixed with or overwrite the frozen study.

Let π denote a nonanticipative policy, s a forecast path, ps its probability and WT,s(π) terminal account wealth. Expected return is

\(G_t(\pi)=\sum_s p_s\,[W_{T,s}(\pi)/W_t-1]\)
\(T=t+4\text{ weeks}.\)

Wealth includes unspent cash and modelled costs. Forecast netSD uses complete terminal account returns including cash and matched reference-asset distributions from the same paths, horizon and probabilities, consistent with the implemented terminal engine. Any invested-holdings-only diagnostic is labelled separately. An all-cash candidate is evaluated as a deterministic account return distribution.

\(S_t(\pi)=\dfrac{\operatorname{OUT}_t(\pi)-\operatorname{IN}_t(\pi)}{D_t(\pi)}\)
\(D_t(\pi)=\max_{j\in\mathcal U\cup\{\pi\}}[\operatorname{OUT}_j+\operatorname{IN}_j].\)

The candidate enters the augmented reference network once. The denominator is recomputed for every candidate and resample; no constant denominator such as 175 is permitted. The reference universe is identical across competing portfolios at a given origin. For a zero augmented-graph denominator, the implemented score is zero; this numerical convention is reported explicitly. netSD is a descriptive network statistic; dominance-edge counts alone are not statistical evidence of population dominance.

Three quantities are reported distinctly: historical IS daily-return netSD, predictive terminal-distribution netSD, and realised OOS daily-return netSD. A four-week forecast distribution and approximately one month of realised daily observations are different estimands. We will not subtract them and label the difference a calibrated forecast error. Like-for-like historical IS/OOS daily scores and supplementary nonoverlapping realised four-week scores will make this distinction explicit. Short OOS tails and dominance scores will carry sample counts and uncertainty warnings.

3.2 Feasible policies and experimental groups

The primary experiment uses STOCHASTIC2 with four decisions at weeks 0, 1, 2 and 3, valuation at week 4 and the C4 branching vector [20,20,10,4]. This gives 4,421 decision nodes and 16,000 terminal paths when all branches are retained. Grouping factors are standard versus A purchase limits and Sale versus No-sale: four groups. Standard caps are EUR 1,000 aggregate purchases and EUR 750 per asset per decision; A removes those two caps. Other financing, eligibility, sector and US constraints remain matched. The matched historical track preserves its sealed US purchase/sales contract. A separate current-inventory track uses the authorised 30% marked direct-US position cap and permits US sales under both policies; the two tracks are not pooled. No-sale retains the registered passive sector-excess allowance, while new trades remain subject to its 30% trade rule. It does not force expenditure of all cash.

Full-tree STOCHASTIC2, two-decision STOCHASTIC and No-initial-sales policies are secondary sensitivity groups after the primary budget gate. Existing comparison portfolios are evaluated on matching dates, constraints and reporting scopes; unmatched permissions are labelled rather than pooled. Frozen-inventory experiments and real-portfolio prospective experiments are separate. A feasible no-trade policy is a baseline when permitted, not a replacement for a solver failure. Infeasibility certificates, numerical failure and an unfinished search are different statuses.

3.3 Pareto construction: matched weighted-sum and ε-constraint methods

The revised NETSD-OOS experiment gives priority to feasible complete portfolios with positive IS-predicted terminal account return. This is a portfolio-level rule, including cash. It does not use realised OOS returns to screen policies. The completed historical P1 archive retains its original eleven thresholds and is not a completed result of this revision.

Let \(\mathcal F_t\) be the feasible policy set at origin \(t\), \(\widehat\mu_t(\pi)\) the IS-predicted terminal account return of policy \(\pi\), and \(\tau=10^{-12}\) the declared numerical positivity threshold. Define

\[\mathcal F_t^+=\{\pi\in\mathcal F_t:\widehat\mu_t(\pi)>\tau\}.\]

If a positive feasible policy is found, all selected grid policies must belong to the positive set. Otherwise nonpositive policies can be evaluated as an explicitly flagged fallback, after the positive-return endpoint search. A heuristic archive with no positive policy does not establish global infeasibility of positive return.

\[\alpha_j=\frac{j}{99},\qquad \varepsilon_{t,j}=\mu_{L,t}+\alpha_j(\mu_{H,t}-\mu_{L,t}),\qquad j=0,\ldots,99.\]

Method A: ε-constraint. There are exactly 100 uniformly spaced threshold indices, including both endpoints. Here \(\mu_{L,t}\) is the return at the maximum-netSD endpoint and \(\mu_{H,t}\) the maximum-return endpoint, both determined on the positive-first eligible set. Optimise netSD subject to each expected-return threshold. Repeated selected policies and a zero endpoint range are recorded; 100 settings do not guarantee 100 distinct portfolios.

Method B: normalised weighted sum. Let \(S_t(\pi)=\operatorname{netSD}_t(\pi)\) and \(G_t(\pi)=\widehat\mu_t(\pi)\). Calibration bounds \(S_t^{min},S_t^{max},G_t^{min},G_t^{max}\) are determined from an IS-only calibration search on the same positive-first feasible set and frozen before the grid sweep. Define

\[\widetilde S_t(\pi)=\frac{S_t(\pi)-S_t^{min}}{S_t^{max}-S_t^{min}},\qquad \widetilde G_t(\pi)=\frac{G_t(\pi)-G_t^{min}}{G_t^{max}-G_t^{min}}.\]
\[\max_{\pi\in\mathcal F_t^*}\big[(1-\lambda_j)\widetilde S_t(\pi)+\lambda_j\widetilde G_t(\pi)\big],\qquad \lambda_j=j/99,\quad j=0,\ldots,99.\]

Here \(\mathcal F_t^*\) denotes the positive-first eligible set or the explicitly flagged fallback set. The return weight is \(\lambda\); \(1-\lambda\) weights netSD. At zero the criterion is netSD only; at one it is expected return only. A criterion with zero calibration range contributes zero. Scores are not clipped. If later search changes the calibration anchors, record a new revision and rerun the complete affected sweep; do not silently change scales between weights. Frozen normalisation bounds do not freeze the candidate-dependent netSD graph denominator.

Both methods use the same origins, IS/OOS splits, feasible instruments, constraints, scenarios, probabilities, costs and matched seeds/search budgets. The threshold index \(\alpha\) and weight \(\lambda\) have different meanings; equal numerical indices are not equivalent economic preferences. Weighted sums can miss unsupported efficient points. Compare attained fronts, selected quantities, common purchases, OOS netSD/return, Sharpe, Sortino, Rachev, CVaR, drawdown, final wealth and computational cost. Any deployable preference selection uses inner IS validation only.

Each method has 100 settings: 200 total per origin/model group/start. The revised primary planning budget is 355,200 outer nominal searches, or 1,420,800 including the three inner chronological tests, before valid cache reuse. These are planned counts, not completed work or measured completion times. The full nested experiment remains separate from the completed eleven-point technical pilot.

3.4 Portfolio composition and stable decisions

Report initial holdings, initial purchases and sales, sector and cash weights, and future path-conditional trades separately. For asset i, let biα be its additional week-0 purchase. Define preference selection frequency fi, the common additional quantity ci and the range:

\(f_i=K^{-1}\sum_{\alpha\in A}\mathbf1\{b_{i\alpha}>\tau_i\}\)
\(c_i=\min_{\alpha\in A}b_{i\alpha}\)
\([b_i^{\min},b_i^{\max}]=[\min_\alpha b_{i\alpha},\max_\alpha b_{i\alpha}].\)

τi is a prespecified executable-quantity/numerical threshold, respecting integer Baltic lots and fractional US precision; it is fixed before external results are inspected. A strict common buy core requires fi=1 and positive ci over the registered complete uniform grid. Show the minimum, median and maximum extra quantities and the sign of the trade. A current holding that cannot be sold does not count as evidence of a stable BUY. Frequencies describe the tested preference range, not probabilities that an asset is intrinsically optimal. Adaptive extra points are displayed separately or integrated with interval weights, never allowed to inflate frequency by oversampling one part of the front.

Weight paths wi(α), cash paths and sector paths reveal concentration changes. Use Jaccard similarity for buy sets, the cash-inclusive L1 distance between compositions, turnover and local sensitivity |Δwi|/|Δα|. All-empty buy sets are labelled no-purchase agreement, not strong asset-selection evidence. Equal supports with substantially different quantities are not equivalent decisions. Multiple starts reveal numerical/search stability; blocked resampling of IS observations or fitted forecast residuals assesses estimation stability. Grid, seed, resampling, temporal and regime stability are stored as separate dimensions. A provisional 90% persistent-buy label is descriptive; strict 100% commonality remains a separate label, with sensitivity at 80%,90%,95% and100% fixed in advance.

For future stages, aggregate purchases along each feasible path, not across incompatible branches. Let Biαs=Σk=1..3bikαs. Report probability of any purchase, scenario-weighted median, maximum, minimum positive and unconditional minimum (which may be zero). Separately report a positive quantity common to all tested preferences and all relevant scenarios, if one exists. Do not call the minimum positive a guaranteed purchase. Compare policies on matched histories or a common independent evaluation-path set; raw node IDs from different trees are not interchangeable.

An additional consensus policy is optional. Componentwise medians or minima can violate budgets, sector limits or self-financing when combined. Construct the implementable consensus by a constrained projection or optimisation, fixing only feasible common quantities, then validate every node. Its selection must use IS/inner validation only. It receives a separate OOS result; the common core alone is not an executable whole portfolio and is not a safety guarantee.

3.5 Chronological IS/OOS design

Use six calendar months of information ending at each weekly origin and one subsequent calendar month as the outer OOS window, with exact existing trading-session mappings sealed in a manifest. Within the six months, use three chronological inner tests: train months1–3/test4, train1–4/test5 and train1–5/test6. These shorter expanding inner fits select the preference rule and tuning parameters; they are not represented as six-month fits. Refit the selected specification on all six months before the untouched outer test. Exclude inner labels or forecast horizons that extend beyond the outer training cutoff. Market regimes used for decisions must be trained/filtered using that cutoff; retrospective full-history labels are diagnostic only.

Construct an OOS preference-performance curve for every fixed registered α and an independently selected rule. The displayed OOS-best α is an explicitly labelled hindsight oracle, excluded from implementable strategy rankings. Since the existing historical OOS results have already informed this proposal, the historical comparison is exploratory. Confirmatory claims require a separately sealed, uninspected future holdout; its dates must be registered before that track begins. Later held-out dates can be descriptive diagnostics but must never tune an earlier origin.

For dynamic policies, subsequent trades use only observations available at their decision week. Define the mapping from observed histories to tree decisions before evaluation, using an IS-only filter and feasible replanning when required. No nearest-future-path or terminal-trajectory knowledge is permitted. Report retrospective fixed-composition IS statistics separately from causal historical policy replay, and from simulated terminal objective values.

3.6 Performance, wealth and statistical comparison

Primary OOS endpoints are mean realised return and netSD. Report final wealth, cumulative return, annualised Sharpe and Sortino, Rachev, volatility, CVaR95, maximum drawdown, turnover, costs, liquidity and cash utilisation. Use identical return conventions, benchmarks and date masks across models. Show before- and after-cost results when the baseline cost assumptions permit this, with a registered cost sensitivity rather than invented execution prices.

Overlapping weekly-origin monthly tests are dependent. Paired calendar-block bootstrap (2,000 metric-only replicates; block-length sensitivity) will provide intervals for performance differences, recomputing netSD networks inside each replicate. Bootstrap the common calendar series or a registered dependence-preserving origin construction; do not treat overlapping folds as independent. Sample support and the number of comparisons are reported; Holm correction applies within prespecified comparison families. Regime subgroup inference must retain dependence and disclose small groups. Quantile interpolation cannot create missing monthly tail information.

For accumulated wealth, simulate one self-financing calendar strategy: at each weekly origin execute its registered initial action, carry holdings and cash forward, and replace older continuation plans under the fixed receding-horizon rule. Realised wealth is Wt+1=Wt(1+rt+1net). Do not multiply overlapping one-month test returns. Repeat baselines under the same continuation rule. Report both horizon-specific final wealth and this sequential strategy's terminal wealth. Distinguish forecasting value from additional capital contributions.

4.3 Complexity and budget accounting

For H scalarisation methods, F origins, P policy groups, K grid points per method, M starts and J inner tests, nominal policy searches are

\(N_{\mathrm{solve}}=HFPKM(J+1).\)

With H=2, F=222, P=4, K=100, M=2 and J=3, the matched primary budget is 1,420,800 nominal policy searches, including 355,200 outer refits. Per method this is 710,400 and 177,600 respectively. A six-origin, two-method pilot with three starts requires 14,400 outer searches, or 57,600 including three inner tests. These are planning counts, not completed work or an ETA. Shared forecasts, endpoints and valid cache reuse are reported separately. The historical P1 technical pilot remains 24 checked finite-archive cases on eleven ε-thresholds.

A rough cost per candidate comprises propagation over N decision nodes/L terminal paths and construction/comparison of candidate and A reference distributions. Sorting costs approximately O((A+1)LlogL); candidate-edge comparisons with sorted references are approximately O(AL) for a fixed number of dominance orders. Reference–reference edges may be cached per origin when unchanged, while candidate incident edges and D(π) must be refreshed. The mixed-integer nonconvex policy search has no polynomial global-solution guarantee; each grid solution can require many candidates. Tree size, candidate count, grid density and nested validation therefore all enter runtime. Kernel speedup cannot be reported as whole-study speedup.

Estimate wall time only after matched pilot measurements. Use completed task timing distributions, dependency-aware queue simulation, cache-hit rates and actual concurrency; publish median and a conservative range. Include download, preprocessing, forecast fitting, optimisation, independent checks, report rendering and publication. Stop expansion at the pilot budget gate if projected cost is excessive; then record an explicit amended protocol, without claiming the omitted experiment was completed.

Every task key includes source hash, origin, training cutoff, inner/outer split, policy, scalarisation method, preference index, frozen normalisation hash, start seed and code revision. Save a completed grid point atomically after independent validation. On reboot, recheck hashes and input archives, preserve failed artefacts and resume only unfinished keys. Keep separate candidate, point, origin and overall completion markers.

Use the common agent_diagnostics.py wrapper for launches. Diagnostic resource samples occur at start/end and every300s for long tasks; safety guards are separate. Existing raw process/dump logs remain private. Measure CPU-package and GPU-board counters where available, plus complete-phase time. Missing counters or reboot intervals remain unknown; they are not zero. Do not sum overlapping parent/child energy or shared-GPU intervals twice. Report gross host cost separately from attributable job estimates. Wall-socket energy and CO₂ estimates require an explicit measurement/model and declared grid-intensity factor.

5. Planned results and presentation

No numerical result is available for this proposed model. The report will contain: (i) predicted and realised return–netSD planes, with no implication that an OOS point remains Pareto efficient; (ii) asset-weight and buy/sell heatmaps across α; (iii) a common-buy core with quantities and five separate stability dimensions; (iv) matched IS/OOS metrics and final-wealth comparisons; (v) regime-specific and cost sensitivities; (vi) CPU/GPU throughput, full-cycle time, uncertainty and measured energy.

The interactive Pareto chart will retain a selected point's name and reveal the complete policy's initial composition, expected return, netSD, cash, constraints and subsequent-trade summary. Common versus preference-sensitive assets receive distinct labels. Tables remain horizontal with compact headers, fit their container at desktop/mobile widths, and sections are collapsible. Each table identifies dates, scope, actual sample size and whether values are forecasts, historical diagnostics or realised tests. Results belong in section3; methods and computational results are linked to the appropriate subsections, without repeating bulky daily controls.

6. Discussion, limitations and conclusions

The proposed design can show whether choosing a point along the return–netSD trade-off matters economically and whether common decisions have useful subsequent properties. Commonality is not proof that a purchase is safe, optimal in all markets or profitable. Heuristic search, finite grids, limited monthly samples, estimated probabilities and historically informed hypothesis design all restrict interpretation. The final conclusion will be written after the registered experiments, including negative findings and computation failures. The current conclusion is a methodological proposal: study the whole attained trade-off, evaluate each deployable selection rule causally and distinguish portfolio persistence from genuine stability of new trades.
