3.5.1. Introduction

Portfolio selection requires decisions based on estimates of future returns, dependence and downside risk. The usefulness of an allocation therefore depends on how those estimates and the resulting trading decisions perform after the estimation period. This distinction is particularly relevant when several optimisation criteria and forecasting models are compared: a favourable fitted score can reflect characteristics of the estimation sample, while subsequent performance also depends on changes in market conditions, transaction costs and the feasibility of the proposed trades. A chronological assessment is consequently necessary to establish the scope and stability of the observed portfolio advantages.

The present study examines this problem for a common investment universe comprising Baltic shares, investment funds and eligible US equities. It compares dominance-based portfolio selection with mean–variance, minimax and robust alternatives, together with forecast-informed stochastic policies. The stochastic specifications differ in decision frequency, scenario-tree structure and purchase-value constraints. Their evaluation must therefore distinguish the quality of an initial allocation from the contribution of subsequent decisions, while retaining each model’s declared trading and concentration restrictions.

The objective is to assess how portfolio performance changes between estimation and subsequent evaluation periods, and whether the relative behaviour of the models is consistent across investment origins and market regimes. Three questions guide the analysis. First, to what extent do favourable in-sample dominance and performance measures persist out of sample? Second, how do risk, wealth accumulation and model feasibility vary with the market conditions prevailing at the investment origin? Third, what incremental performance is associated with deploying later stochastic decisions using the information available at the time, compared with retaining the fitted initial allocation?

To address these questions, the study uses rolling investment origins separated by one week. Each model is refitted on the preceding six calendar months and evaluated over the following calendar month. A further origin-specific evaluation follows the remaining holdings and cash through the available history after that month. The comparison jointly considers netSD, mean return, volatility, Sharpe and Sortino ratios, the Rachev ratio, drawdown, conditional value-at-risk and accumulated wealth. Transaction costs, turnover and constraint feasibility provide additional evidence on the practical implications of the allocations.

The contribution is a common empirical framework linking portfolio optimisation, information-adapted deployment and subsequent performance across repeated chronological evaluations. Interpretation is conditional on the study’s frozen investment universe, inventory and original constraint specification. Weekly origins produce overlapping evaluation periods, so the comparisons are descriptive and dependent; their results establish the behaviour of the specified historical experiment and provide a basis for further prospective validation.

3.5.2. Literature review

3.5.2.1. Portfolio theory and the distinction between optimisation and evaluation

Modern portfolio theory treats investment risk as a property of the joint distribution of asset returns. In Markowitz’s formulation, diversification depends on covariance as well as individual volatility, and efficient allocations balance expected return against portfolio variance. A security’s contribution therefore depends on the other holdings and their weights. The efficient frontier describes the opportunities implied by the assumed inputs and constraints; its interpretation depends on how those inputs were obtained. An allocation can be efficient under an estimated distribution while delivering disappointing subsequent results. Markowitz (1952) thus provides the allocation framework, while empirical validation addresses the reliability of its application.

Expected returns and covariance matrices must be estimated. Optimisation can amplify small estimation errors by assigning large weights to assets that appear unusually attractive in the available sample. DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal compare fourteen allocation models across seven datasets and find that none consistently dominates equal weighting on their reported out-of-sample criteria. This is evidence about the difficulty of overcoming estimation risk, rather than a universal prescription for equal weights. Jagannathan and Ma show that nonnegative weight constraints can reduce estimation-induced risk. These findings motivate simple benchmarks and constrained alternatives, whose advantages still require subsequent evaluation. DeMiguel et al. (2009); Jagannathan and Ma (2003).

3.5.2.2. What full-history performance can establish

Full-history analysis remains valuable for describing realised returns, drawdowns, correlations, concentration and exposure to particular episodes. A fixed-composition historical chart answers how a specified basket would have behaved over observed prices, subject to its accounting assumptions. An optimisation using all available observations identifies an allocation favoured by that fitted empirical distribution. These are legitimate descriptive questions. They differ from evaluating a decision rule that had to select investments before the subsequent returns became available. In this report, full-period diagnostics and chronological validation are consequently complementary sources of evidence. A backtest can cover the full available span and still include valid out-of-sample evaluations when each decision is reconstructed from prior information and the evaluation design is fixed in advance. The decisive issue is which observations informed each decision and model choice.

The distinction becomes concrete when a portfolio is selected using returns from 2021–2026 and then evaluated on those same years. Its weights incorporate information from the entire interval, including events occurring after the proposed investment date. Changing the objective from variance to netSD or tail risk leaves this information problem in place. Even without an explicit optimiser, selecting assets, technical thresholds or scenario settings after inspecting their historical outcomes can favour chance patterns. White’s analysis of data snooping explains why reusing a dataset for specification search changes the interpretation of the best observed result. A longer reused sample does not create an independent evaluation period. White (2000).

3.5.2.3. In-sample, out-of-sample and rolling-origin designs

In-sample evaluation measures behaviour on observations available during estimation. It is useful for diagnosing fitted relationships, numerical feasibility and model sensitivity. Out-of-sample evaluation applies a fitted specification to subsequent observations excluded from that fit. The separation must include the complete estimation pipeline: transformations, forecasting parameters, regime models, clustering and allocation decisions. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos describe rolling-origin evaluation as repeated forecasting from successive information cutoffs. This establishes the relevant chronology: a forecast is judged against observations occurring after its own training period. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos (2021, Chapter 5).

A single chronological split can be strongly influenced by its chosen market episode. Repeated origins reveal whether performance changes with the investment date. Cerqueira, Torgo and Mozetič compare validation procedures across real and synthetic time series, finding that their relative reliability depends on the data-generating setting; repeated holdouts perform particularly well in their non-stationary experiments. Their results support examining several temporally ordered tests, while providing no universal justification for this report’s exact window lengths. The present six-month estimation window, one-month test and weekly shift are declared experimental choices. Their robustness would require additional, separately specified comparisons. Cerqueira et al. (2020).

Rolling and expanding estimation windows also answer different questions. A rolling window drops older observations, allowing adaptation while retaining fewer data for estimation. An expanding window retains the growing history, potentially stabilising estimates but increasing exposure to older conditions. There is no automatic guarantee that either choice performs best for every asset or objective. For the present design, six months supply relatively recent information, but can contain few observations of a stressed regime. A one-month test offers repeated feedback while producing noisy tail estimates. These are design trade-offs to be examined in Results, rather than conclusions established by the literature.

3.5.2.4. Temporal dependence, overlapping tests and information leakage

Time-series validation requires attention to dependence and the information set. Randomly distributing observations across training and test sets can admit later information into an earlier decision. However, the statement that ordinary cross-validation is always invalid for time series is too broad. Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo establish conditions under which standard cross-validation is valid for autoregressive prediction with uncorrelated errors. Those conditions must be assessed for the intended model and task. Here, chronological evaluation is directly aligned with the decision problem, including published-price lags and sequential trading. The methodological argument concerns this deployment setting, rather than a blanket prohibition of every alternative validation method. Bergmeir et al. (2018).

Weekly origins with one-month tests produce overlapping evaluation periods. Adjacent portfolios experience many of the same market days, so their outcomes are dependent. The general econometric problem of sampling more frequently than the forecast horizon is central to Hansen and Hodrick’s work on multi-period forecasts. Applied here, overlap means that the number of origins is not the number of independent market experiments. It also prevents multiplying successive overlapping monthly returns into one investor’s wealth path. A continuous strategy would require a separate specification of how simultaneous allocations share capital. Paired, dependence-aware inference would be needed before interpreting a difference in average performance as statistically established. Hansen and Hodrick (1980).

3.5.2.5. Model selection, survivorship and the limits of a holdout

An evaluation period can cease to function as an independent test when its results repeatedly guide model redesign. Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu examine this problem through the probability of backtest overfitting and propose combinatorially symmetric cross-validation as a diagnostic. Their analysis highlights how selecting the most attractive result from many configurations can produce misleading expectations. For this report, comparing several optimisers, sale policies, scenario structures and performance measures creates a model-selection issue even when each individual fit respects chronology. A final claim about a selected winner would require an additional evaluation whose outcomes did not influence that selection. The probability of backtest overfitting itself has not been estimated here. Bailey et al. (2017).

The composition of the historical dataset matters as well. Brown, Goetzmann, Ibbotson and Ross demonstrate that survivorship selection can generate apparent persistence in performance. A study restricted to instruments available today may omit investments that disappeared or became ineligible. In the present experiment, the universe and initial inventory are retrospective fixed assumptions, so chronological refitting cannot establish that the same opportunity set was available to a historical investor. Similarly, origin-specific post-OOS tails have different lengths, and a later origin may train on observations that were held out at an earlier origin. These tails extend each origin’s assessment, while the overall design retains no globally untouched final sample. Brown et al. (1992).

3.5.2.6. Performance criteria, trading costs and sequential decisions

Portfolio quality is multidimensional. Mean return and accumulated wealth describe growth; volatility and drawdown describe different aspects of risk. The Sharpe ratio summarises reward relative to variability, but its estimation and temporal aggregation depend on return properties. Lo shows that serial correlation can materially affect Sharpe-ratio inference and annualisation. Downside and tail criteria add further information: Rockafellar and Uryasev develop portfolio optimisation based on conditional value-at-risk. Their work motivates examining losses in adverse outcomes alongside average performance. In a one-month sample, however, a five-percent tail may be represented by roughly one observation, making tail comparisons particularly sensitive to individual days. Lo (2002); Rockafellar and Uryasev (2000).

The link to implementation also requires explicit accounting. Turnover, transaction costs, cash and binding constraints can change the outcome of an otherwise attractive allocation. This report therefore evaluates returns together with trading costs and feasibility. Its netSD measure additionally depends on the comparison network and candidate-specific denominator; agreement between a scenario-law objective and a realised historical score cannot be presumed. For stochastic portfolios, the initial decision and the subsequent policy are separate objects of evaluation. Later trades must use information available at their execution date. Reporting initial-only allocations and observed-prefix deployments separately makes the contribution of later decisions assessable within the stated historical experiment.

3.5.2.7. Implications for the present empirical study

Taken together, the literature motivates chronological refitting, transparent benchmarks, explicit investment constraints and evaluation across several performance dimensions. Full-history analysis provides context and diagnostics; rolling tests assess subsequent behaviour under repeated information cutoffs. The present study combines these roles while retaining the frozen research inventory and original model contract. Results should be interpreted through sample coverage, regime conditions, costs and deployment rules, with mathematically infeasible cases disclosed separately. A consistently favourable descriptive comparison would justify further investigation and prospective testing. The strength of any claim about generalisable investment performance depends on an additional evaluation protected from the choices made during this research.

3.5.3. Methodology

3.5.3.1. Research design, estimand and methodological contribution

The experiment evaluates the subsequent behaviour of portfolio-selection procedures fitted to a limited, recent information set. Its unit of analysis is an investment origin, with a separate estimation sample, fitted allocation or policy, execution ledger and evaluation period. At each origin, all methods receive the same frozen initial inventory and origin-specific cash account. Repeating this experiment across origins permits comparison of estimation-period performance, subsequent performance and sensitivity to the market conditions prevailing when the investment decision was made. The design follows chronological evaluation principles discussed in Hyndman and Athanasopoulos and Cerqueira et al..

The contribution of this implementation is the integration of origin-specific refitting, regime-conditioned scenario generation, complete stochastic-policy verification and information-adapted historical deployment within one auditable comparison. It connects model feasibility and execution to the resulting wealth and risk measures. The calendar-window design, HMMs, mean–variance optimisation and holdout principle are established methods. The study makes no priority claim for a new general optimisation algorithm or validation theorem. Its methodological value lies in the explicit connection between the fitted decision problem and the historical procedure used to evaluate that decision.

3.5.3.2. Frozen universe, benchmark and information availability

The sealed configuration contains 72 allocation-input instruments and a broader 116-instrument historical reference universe. Allocation inputs include Baltic shares, funds, PFE and NVIDIA; instrument-specific bounds determine whether additional purchases are permitted. Secondary List instruments are reference-only for new investment. The wider reference panel supports historical dominance comparisons and does not automatically enlarge the purchase universe. Asset identities, sector exposures and the initial inventory are retrospective design assumptions fixed across origins. In particular, the study retains VLP 117, PFE 5.491688, NVIDIA 0.28401 and Technology 7.108 units. Subsequent account purchases and the later production-model US-sale exception do not alter this completed experiment.

Two price panels separate valuation from assumed decision-time information. Valuation prices provide the observed marks used to reconstruct wealth. Information prices lag fund NAVs by two common-calendar sessions and US shares by one; Baltic share marks have zero additional lag. Available observations are carried forward onto the common Baltic-session grid, with no pre-inception price backfill. The resulting information rule is

\[\widetilde P_{i,t}=P^{v}_{i,t-\ell_i},\qquad \ell_i\in\{0,1,2\}.\]

Here, Pv denotes the valuation mark, the tilde denotes the information mark, and the lag depends on instrument type. Market-index inputs used to estimate regimes are additionally restricted to observations dated at least two business days before the origin. These availability conventions prevent the declared fitting procedure from using later marks. They remain assumptions about historical availability: the dataset does not reconstruct every past NAV publication timestamp, delisting or change in investment eligibility. All inputs are expressed in EUR using the retained source conversions.

3.5.3.3. Calendar windows and independent origin accounts

Candidate origins advance by seven calendar days and are mapped backwards to an observed session. Duplicate mapped origins are discarded. For origin tk, estimation uses observations strictly after the date six calendar months earlier and through the origin. Testing uses the following calendar month, ending at its last available observed session. Only complete monthly tests enter; preparation requires at least 90 training price observations and 15 test observations. The implemented partition is

\[\mathcal I_k=(t_k-6\,\mathrm{months},t_k].\]
\[\mathcal O_k=(t_k,t_k+1\,\mathrm{month}].\]
\[\mathcal H_k=(\max\mathcal O_k,T].\]

The post-test tail extends from the end of that origin’s OOS month to the data endpoint, T = 17 November 2026. The sealed schedule comprises 222 origins from 20 July 2022 to 14 October 2026. The tail assesses persistence after within-month trading has ended. Its length depends on the origin, and later origins may train on observations belonging to an earlier tail. It is therefore an origin-specific held-out evaluation, with no globally untouched final test set.

The capital ceiling is B = EUR 15,000. At each origin, cash is reset from the fixed quantities and information prices:

\[c^k_0=B-\sum_iq_i^{\mathrm{Current}}\widetilde P_{i,t_k}.\]

An inventory exceeding the ceiling would be reported as infeasible, without silently scaling its positions. Because the outer ledger marks holdings at contemporaneous valuation prices, its initial wealth can differ from EUR 15,000 when information marks are lagged. Methods share the same initial marked wealth within an origin. Accounts at different origins are separate experiments; their overlapping monthly returns are never compounded into a fictitious continuous investment account.

3.5.3.4. Origin-specific estimation and regime construction

Each origin receives an isolated training workspace. Its model inputs end at the declared cutoff, and later valuation observations are read by the outer evaluator. Returns, allocation bounds, liquidity features, forecasting parameters, regime models and scenario contexts are rebuilt from that training information. Completed production allocations are not imported as historical warm starts. The initial inventory supplies a starting candidate where it is feasible. This separation makes the information cutoff inspectable through retained files and hashes.

Regime estimation uses two-state Gaussian hidden Markov models for the Baltic and World index ensemble, with own-history and lagged cross-index specifications. The higher-volatility state is labelled stress. Fits use six initialisations and a minimum effective state mass of 20 observations. An explicitly recorded short-window recovery retries the World intercept-only specification with 30 initialisations and a six-observation floor when necessary. At least three valid Baltic indices and a valid World fit are required. Failed members remain documented. Valid specifications receive equal weight within an index, and indices receive equal regional weight.

Filtered regional stress probabilities define four soft Baltic–World memberships through products of calm and stress probabilities. This is a product approximation to joint membership, rather than an estimated four-state joint HMM. Hard descriptive labels use a 0.3 stress threshold: R1 denotes both regions calm, R2 Baltic stress, R3 World stress and R4 simultaneous stress. Historical regime-netSD allocations use retrospective regime labels estimated within their training window. Those labels describe that fitted sample; they are not reconstructed real-time classifications of every past session. Future regime evaluation retains the origin-fitted parameters and advances filtered probabilities forward. Future smoothed probabilities do not determine historical trades.

Asset forecasts combine four origin-fitted components: daily regime means, daily regime autoregression, weekly regime means and weekly regime autoregression. Autoregressive features retain chronological lags; regime coefficients are regularised and shrunk toward the pooled fit with strength governed by effective regime mass and a 60-observation shrinkage constant. The four forecast components receive equal weight in this experiment. Weekly fitting requires at least 20 observed weekly prices; scenario construction requires 16 joint weekly residuals or 40 daily residuals. These declared short-window settings differ from long-history production eligibility.

3.5.3.5. Portfolio treatments and common constraints

The comparison includes Current, Markowitz, Minimax, Robust, Update2, Update3, full-training netSD and independently fitted regime-netSD allocations, alongside Stochastic, Stochastic 2 and their A variants. Current retains the frozen inventory without new trades. A fully available origin contains 33 fitted initial portfolios; adding observed-prefix deployments for the 12 stochastic model-policy combinations yields up to 45 deployment cases. Regime-specific fits lacking usable observations are explicitly unavailable. These treatments contrast covariance-sensitive risk, worst-sample outcomes, tail-risk penalties, dominance-based selection and adaptation through later decisions. Trading permissions and purchase caps provide additional treatment dimensions. Keeping these differences explicit allows an observed advantage to be interpreted in relation to the problem that generated it.

Standard stochastic models impose gross purchase ceilings at every decision. For a node n, positive quantity changes are measured relative to its parent:

\[\sum_iP_{i,n}(q_{i,n}-q_{i,pa(n)})_+\le1000.\]
\[P_{i,n}(q_{i,n}-q_{i,pa(n)})_+\le750.\]

The A variants remove both purchase-value ceilings. Cash nonnegativity, absence of short positions, sector exposure limits, instrument bounds and lot rules remain. Each modeled node is subject to a 30% sector limit measured against total marked wealth, including cash. Native stochastic checks also retain the fitted historical expected-return floor relative to Current, a maximum of four held funds, minimum sizes for positions absent from initial Current and the cumulative EUR 1,000 US-purchase allowance along a scenario path. Holding cash is permitted; budget exhaustion is never imposed as a requirement.

Sale permits reductions at all decisions; No-sale forbids them throughout the frozen study; No-initial-sales forbids initial reductions and permits subsequent sales. The later production rule allowing US sales under every policy is a different model contract. Preserving the original contract maintains comparability across the completed origins. Original model-specific penalty and feasibility settings are retained rather than silently replaced by one common objective.

3.5.3.6. Scenario trees, optimisation and attainable policies

The native two-stage model has decisions at steps 0 and 10, followed by terminal valuation at step 20, with 20 joint scenarios. The weekly model decides at steps 0, 5, 10 and 15 and values the portfolio at step 20. Its C4 branching configuration is 20 × 20 × 10 × 4, giving 16,000 terminal outcomes and 4,421 decision nodes including the root. Reduction aggregates later branches within regimes and checks preservation of regime probability mass. The final branching produces terminal values, with no additional trade at the valuation date.

For each native stochastic specification, the search evaluates complete terminal portfolio returns, including remaining cash, under its fitted scenario probabilities. The criterion combines terminal-law dominance and the model’s declared expected penalties:

\[J_{k,m}(\pi)=\operatorname{netSD}^{\mathrm{terminal}}_{k,m}(\pi)-\mathbb E_{k,m}[\Pi_m(\pi)].\]

Here, π is a complete policy and Π contains the native preference penalties. netSD is recomputed for the candidate portfolio with its augmented-network denominator. Exact evaluation refers to the specified discrete scenario law; it does not certify that the law describes future markets or that the best possible policy has been found. The search varies initial holdings and structured recourse rules through a bounded heuristic procedure. Feasible inclusions and targeted recovery can improve its attained result. Global optimality is not certified.

The weekly tree shares decisions among scenarios with the same observed history. The original two-stage formulation retains its stronger full-scenario-revelation assumption at recourse. Consequently, native terminal scores from the two formulations belong to different information structures and scenario laws. Their historical comparison requires a separately specified implementable deployment, described next.

3.5.3.7. Historical deployment from the observed prefix

Every stochastic fit is evaluated in two ways. Initial-only deployment executes the fitted first allocation and subsequently retains its holdings and cash. Observed-prefix deployment additionally applies later fitted decisions using the information available at each scheduled decision. The weekly replay compares the current observed endpoint with children of the previously selected node. Distances use log-price changes standardised by training-period asset volatility. Restricting the search to those children preserves the previously selected history. The original two-stage replay instead matches the already observed trajectory prefix among the fitted scenarios.

The selected node supplies the fitted recourse signal and rule. A recovered explicit-node policy supplies its stored quantity vector. Actual future observations beyond the decision cutoff never enter node selection. This projection is an approximation for histories outside the fitted tree; it does not reproduce perfect-information recourse. Weekly decisions are scheduled after 5, 10 and 15 observed steps and two-stage recourse after 10. Their requested orders are processed at the next observed valuation. After the last fill, quantities and cash remain fixed through the remaining month and the post-OOS tail.

3.5.3.8. Paper execution, costs and realised constraint exposure

Paper execution sells permitted reductions first, then scales requested purchases to available cash, gross purchase limits, remaining US-purchase capacity and sector headroom. Purchases are rounded down to instrument steps: whole Baltic shares, 0.001 fund units and 0.000001 US-share units. Opening positions must satisfy the retained minimum of ten Baltic shares or EUR 300 for a fund. Unfilled quantities and rejected purchases are recorded. This procedure evaluates orders against the next observed marks without assuming guaranteed market liquidity or a historical broker fill.

The account is self-financing. With proportional execution charge κ, its cash and marked wealth satisfy

\[\Delta c_t=-\sum_iP^v_{i,t}\Delta q_{i,t}-\kappa\sum_iP^v_{i,t}|\Delta q_{i,t}|.\]
\[V_t=c_t+\sum_iq_{i,t}P^v_{i,t}.\]

Execution overlays use 0, 10, 25 and 50 basis points, with 10 basis points as the primary reported case. The fitted policy is retained across these overlays; it is not reoptimised separately for each fee level. Cash earns zero. Cash dividends, taxes, bid–ask spreads and market impact are outside the retained wealth calculation.

The distinction between modeled feasibility and realised exposure is explicit. The 30% bound is checked at every fitted decision node. During historical replay, purchases are restricted by observed sector headroom, but market movements between decisions can produce marked exposure breaches. The ledger records those breaches and retains the affected paths; it does not impose continuous compulsory liquidation. Results therefore assess the implemented replay and cannot establish continuous realised compliance with the sector ceiling. A strict automatic-rebalancing evaluation would be a separate experiment.

3.5.3.9. Independent verification and treatment of infeasibility

Each completed fit is subject to independent checks of its input hashes, scenario probabilities, node decisions, cash, quantities and constraints. Historical checks reconstruct ledger returns, fees, turnover and accumulated wealth, verify training cutoffs and match period-specific observation counts. Forward-regime checks confirm that extending evaluation leaves already filtered training-prefix probabilities unchanged. These gates distinguish a completed numerical output from a verified research observation.

A failed heuristic search alone does not prove that the mathematical problem is infeasible. Recovery therefore attempts additional feasible seeds, sector-repair rules and, where required, integer-feasibility search under the same constraints. Certified infeasibility requires an independently reconstructed contradiction in a necessary continuous relaxation, using a nonnegative combination of inequalities as a Farkas witness. Such a certificate establishes that the original constrained policy cannot exist. Infeasible cases receive no substitute return or fabricated zero. Recovery methods and search limits are retained in the audit; a solver time limit with a valid incumbent establishes feasibility, without establishing objective optimality. The recovery algorithm differs across the explicitly recorded affected origins.

3.5.3.10. Performance measurement, netSD and uncertainty

\[V_t=c_t+\sum_iq_{i,t}P_{i,t},\quad r_t=V_t/V_{t-1}-1,\quad \bar r=\frac1n\sum_t r_t,\quad s_r^2=\frac{\sum_t(r_t-\bar r)^2}{n-1}\]
\[SR=\bar r/s_r,\quad SoR=\bar r/\sqrt{n^{-1}\sum_t\min(r_t,0)^2},\quad MDD=\max_t\left(1-\frac{V_t}{\max_{u\le t}V_u}\right)\]

Sharpe and Sortino use zero risk-free/target returns; annualised ratios multiply by √252. Rachev divides the upper 5% fractional tail mean by minus the lower 5% mean; CVaR95 is minus that lower mean. A monthly 5% tail is often approximately one observation. Undefined ratios remain unavailable. IS curves are fitted origin weights rebased at the training start, not historical executed trades.

\[\operatorname{netSD}(p)=\frac{d_p^{out}-d_p^{in}}{\max_{v\in G\cup\{p\}}(d_v^{out}+d_v^{in})}\]

Every daily empirical netSD uses the matched 116-asset reference network plus Current and a candidate-dependent augmented denominator. These IS/OOS/held-out daily-network values differ from the native four-week terminal-law objective, which is exported separately. No component-weighted netSD shortcut is used.

Overlapping one-month windows are dependent. Their monthly returns are never compounded into a fictitious continuous account. The accumulated wealth chart is one selected origin's self-financing trajectory. The held-out periods have different lengths. Comparisons are descriptive; the added exploratory intervals use paired dependence-aware resampling as specified in 3.5.3.12, and model selection requires an additional untouched test. No IID p-values or purported winner are supplied.

3.5.3.11. Comparison strategy and interpretation

In-sample curves use the origin-fitted initial risky and cash weights rebased at the beginning of the training interval. They are fitted diagnostics, not reconstructed training-period executions. OOS and tail curves come from their self-financing ledgers. Subsequent metrics, including daily-network netSD, are calculated on matched period observations as defined in 3.5.3.10. Native terminal-law scores are reported separately. This separation prevents a fitted scenario objective from being presented as an observed subsequent performance measure.

Across origins, the dashboard reports descriptive means, sample standard deviations, finite sample counts and paired differences against Current. Available-case means can cover different origins; the common-origin option restricts comparison to origins with every native model feasible. Overlapping windows and unequal tail lengths remain visible limitations. No independent-observation significance test, probability-of-backtest-overfitting estimate or globally untouched winner-selection result is claimed. The resulting evidence concerns the stability, feasibility and subsequent behaviour of the declared portfolio procedures under a common retrospective design, providing a documented basis for further prospective evaluation.

3.5.3.12. Exploratory paired inference and sensitivity design

The added inferential analysis compares origin-level metrics on the 157 origins where all 45 deployments are fitted. Pairwise finite observations define each contrast; the full 222-position origin grid is retained, including gaps. The 218-origin native-common set and all available paired observations provide coverage sensitivity. The analysis plan was fixed before computing these tests, after the original portfolio study had completed. It is an exploratory addition rather than preregistration of the original experiment.

\[d_k=M_k^{case}-M_k^{reference},\qquad \bar d=N^{-1}\sum_{k\in A}d_k.\]

Here, A is the set of finite paired origins and N is its size. Tests concern the mean difference in an origin-level metric. In particular, the mean of monthly Sharpe ratios is a different estimand from a Sharpe ratio computed on a continuous return account. Positive differences favour the case for return and dominance measures; negative differences favour it for STD, drawdown and CVaR.

A circular block bootstrap resamples consecutive positions of the full origin grid, with wraparound and common block draws across cases. Each of 19,999 replications recomputes the finite paired mean. The primary block is 30 origins, reflecting roughly six months of training overlap and one month of test overlap; 4, 8, 13, 26 and 52 are prespecified sensitivity settings for this analysis. Missing comparisons remain missing. Recorded metric vectors are resampled; the portfolio models are not re-estimated inside each bootstrap replication. This targets uncertainty in the observed mean contrasts under a sufficiently stable, weakly dependent finite-moment origin process. Structural changes and unstable tail ratios remain limitations. See Politis and Romano (1991).

Basic 95% intervals invert the centred bootstrap deviations. The two-sided bootstrap test uses a plus-one finite-replication correction, with the replication count denoted by Brep:

\[p^*=\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{B_{\mathrm{rep}}}\mathbf1\{|\bar d_b^*-\bar d|\ge|\bar d|\}}{B_{\mathrm{rep}}+1}.\]

A Bartlett-kernel Newey-West HAC standard error supplies an asymptotic-normal cross-check at the corresponding lag. Missing grid positions have zero score contributions in the covariance calculation; they are not assigned zero portfolio returns. Thirty paired origins are required for a reported inferential result. Degenerate differences retain an explicit status. See Newey and West (1987).

Holm adjustment is applied across the planned contrasts and all nine outcomes within each declared family, population and dependence setting. The primary against-Current family has 396 tests; later decisions and purchase caps each have 108; sale permissions have 234. Unavailable tests retain their planned slots conservatively. Displayed 95% intervals are marginal, not simultaneous. A headline supported difference requires both the adjusted bootstrap and adjusted HAC p values below 0.05. The separate families remain exploratory; multiplicity adjustment cannot remove adaptive study design or establish an untouched-test winner. See Holm (1979).

3.5.4.2. Coverage and comparison basis

All 222 scheduled origins were audited. The native searches supplied 2,655 independently checked policies; 9 model/origin problems were mathematically certified infeasible. The 218-origin native-common set establishes availability of the twelve native stochastic fits. It does not establish availability of every regime-specific historical allocation. All 33 initial portfolios and 45 deployment cases are jointly available at 157 origins; this stricter common-fit set is the primary comparison population.

The remaining 65 origins are retained in available-case and native-common sensitivity analyses. No missing result is replaced by a return of zero. Metric-specific undefined ratios further reduce finite paired counts, which are displayed in every table. Within the primary set, origin regimes are R1:27, R2:36, R3:30 and R4:64; the complete schedule has 27,45,44 and 106 origins respectively. A common-fit comparison is conditional on those origins and can differ from the broader historical experience.

Primary OOS results use ten-basis-point paper execution. Means and sample SDs are calculated across origin-level metrics. These are overlapping monthly experiments; wealth observations are not multiplied together to manufacture one cumulative account. Native terminal-law objective scores remain separate from the daily historical-network netSD reported here.

3.5.4.3. Final wealth: comparison of all portfolio methods

Final wealth is the account value at the selected experiment endpoint, including remaining cash and execution costs. For OOS it is the value at the monthly test end; for HELD_OUT it is the value at the fixed data endpoint after that test. IS is a fitted, rebased diagnostic. Each row reports separate origin accounts, never a product of overlapping monthly tests. The previously exported accumulated-wealth endpoint is used directly; no historical result is recalculated or renamed as a new experiment.

3.5.4.4. In-sample, out-of-sample and post-OOS behaviour

Fitted dominance gains do not persist uniformly. Stochastic 2 No-sale has mean historical netSD 0.5544 in sample and 0.4366 out of sample, compared with Current at 0.4967 and 0.4409. Its observed-prefix deployment reaches OOS netSD 0.4379. Thus an estimation-period advantage cannot be read as a realised dominance advantage.

The IS curves are origin-fitted initial allocations rebased over their estimation period. They are fitted diagnostics; observed-prefix IS executions were not manufactured. Post-OOS tails freeze the remaining quantities and cash and vary in length. Their means provide a persistence diagnostic, rather than a matched-horizon significance test. The comparison chart and table below allow all exported metrics and portfolios to be inspected with their finite sample counts.

3.5.4.5. Paired statistical evidence against Current

The largest primary mean OOS netSD is attained by NETSD / SALE at 0.4425, versus Current at 0.4409. The paired difference is 0.0016, with a basic95% block-bootstrap interval [-0.0204,0.0201] and Holm-adjusted bootstrap p=1.000. No deployment demonstrates a positive netSD difference supported by both the primary bootstrap and HAC procedures after multiplicity adjustment. One netSD deterioration is supported. Absence of a detected advantage does not establish equivalence.

The primary Current-comparison family contains 396 tests:44 contrasts across nine outcomes. Five deployments show positive mean-return and end-wealth differences supported by both adjusted procedures. None establishes an adjusted Sharpe, Sortino or Rachev improvement. Three reduce CVaR and maximum drawdown, and four reduce STD; many more increase these risk measures. For example, Markowitz Sale reduces mean OOS daily STD from 0.249% to 0.125% and mean monthly maximum drawdown from 1.038% to 0.509%. Its mean-return advantage is not established. Risk reduction alone does not establish improved risk-adjusted performance.

The largest supported mean end-wealth difference is stoch2A / SALE [observed-prefix]: EUR97.51, interval EUR[53.08,144.34], adjusted bootstrap p=0.0290. Its mean OOS drawdown is 1.945%, against Current’s 1.038%. This illustrates the return–risk trade-off; it is not an untouched-test winner. Wealth differences refer to a mean monthly origin account.

3.5.4.6. Trading permissions, purchase caps and later decisions

The secondary paired families compare later decisions with the same fitted initial-only case, A variants with their standard counterpart, and Sale or No-initial-sales with No-sale. Each contrast retains its model, origin and deployment where applicable. These are attained-policy comparisons: heuristic search and projection error remain part of the implemented procedure, so they do not isolate a population-level causal effect of one constraint.

In the primary set, neither the later-decision family nor the Sale-permission family establishes a netSD improvement after adjustment by both methods. Removing purchase caps establishes one netSD deterioration: Stochastic A No-initial-sales with observed-prefix deployment loses about 0.0481 against its standard counterpart. The associated bootstrap Holm p is 0.0054 and HAC Holm p is 0.0015. Later decisions and uncapped purchases more frequently change risk than they establish risk-adjusted gains. Use the contrast-family filter above to inspect every effect, including directions and unavailable ratios.

The three sale labels refer to the original research contract. The later live-production US-sale exception was not retroactively introduced into these fits. A variants remove both stage-purchase ceilings while retaining the other frozen constraints.

Regime comparisons use the 157-origin common-fit population throughout this panel. They estimate within-regime paired descriptive differences against Current using the origin’s filtered regime classification. They are not additional independent significance tests. Sample sizes differ across regimes, particularly R1 with 27 origins; a pooled ordering can therefore conceal regime dependence. 3.5.4.8. Transaction-cost sensitivity

Execution overlays reuse the fitted policy at 0, 10, 25 and 50 basis points, while retaining cash, rounding and purchase headroom. Thus costs can affect filled quantities as well as fees. The cost panel compares cumulative OOS return, daily return, risk or wealth as available in the original exports. Historical netSD was exported only for the primary ten-basis-point case; no non-primary cost-netSD values are invented. For a netSD selection the cost chart therefore shows cumulative return and states the substitution. With All selected, representative Current, Stochastic2 No-sale and Stochastic2A Sale paths are shown; every deployment is available through the portfolio selector.

3.5.4.9. Dependence and coverage sensitivity

The primary 30-week block was fixed before computing this added inferential analysis, to reflect the six-month training overlap and one-month test overlap. Blocks4,8,13,26 and52 are sensitivity settings, not a search for the smallest p value. The HAC cross-check uses the corresponding Bartlett lag. Full origin-grid positions are retained when a comparison is unavailable; missing outcomes are never imputed as returns of zero. Available and native-common populations show how the coverage decision changes effects. At most a few long blocks fit into this history, so large-block uncertainty is itself imprecise.

All tests are exploratory and conditional on a sufficiently stable weakly dependent origin-metric process with finite moments. Structural change, unstable short-window tail ratios, adaptive research choices and the frozen retrospective universe limit generalisation. Holm correction controls the declared family given valid component p values; it does not remove those design limitations. P values use 19,999 bootstrap replicates and a plus-one correction, giving a minimum raw value of 0.00005 rather than zero.

3.5.4.10. Feasibility and marked historical sector exposure

Native feasibility and realised marked exposure answer different questions. The fitted policies satisfy their verified modeled-node constraints, with infeasibility certificates reported separately. In the historical replay, at least one case has an OOS marked-sector breach at 218 of the 222 origins; the largest recorded weight is 34.61%. These observations include Current and all available deployment cases. They are retained rather than discarded. The replay restricts purchases but does not impose continuous compulsory liquidation after every price change.

Consequently, this study cannot support a claim that every realised path continuously satisfies the 30% ceiling. A strict continuous-rebalancing study would require another declared execution protocol. This is a limitation of the existing experiment, not a proposed relaxation for live trading.

3.5.5. Conclusions and limitations

The common-origin comparison shows that fitted netSD advantages do not establish a subsequent netSD improvement. No deployment demonstrates a positive primary OOS netSD difference against Current supported by both dependence-aware procedures after Holm adjustment. Some A deployments increase mean return and wealth while also increasing risk; classical Sale allocations can reduce risk without establishing superior risk-adjusted performance. No adjusted Sharpe, Sortino or Rachev improvement against Current is established.

These findings favour a multidimensional interpretation over selecting the largest fitted or realised score. Their scope is the frozen retrospective experiment and the stated coverage, costs and deployment rules. Dependence assumptions, short monthly tails, heuristic recovery and recorded historical sector breaches limit stronger claims. Further prospective evaluation and, where required, a strict realised-sector-rebalancing protocol remain necessary before treating a selected procedure as a validated live trading strategy.
