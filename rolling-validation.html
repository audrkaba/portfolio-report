3.5.1. Introduction

Portfolio selection requires decisions based on estimates of future returns, dependence and downside risk. The usefulness of an allocation therefore depends on how those estimates and the resulting trading decisions perform after the estimation period. This distinction is particularly relevant when several optimisation criteria and forecasting models are compared: a favourable fitted score can reflect characteristics of the estimation sample, while subsequent performance also depends on changes in market conditions, transaction costs and the feasibility of the proposed trades. A chronological assessment is consequently necessary to establish the scope and stability of the observed portfolio advantages.

The present study examines this problem for a common investment universe comprising Baltic shares, investment funds and eligible US equities. It compares dominance-based portfolio selection with mean–variance, minimax and robust alternatives, together with forecast-informed stochastic policies. The stochastic specifications differ in decision frequency, scenario-tree structure and purchase-value constraints. Their evaluation must therefore distinguish the quality of an initial allocation from the contribution of subsequent decisions, while retaining each model’s declared trading and concentration restrictions.

The objective is to assess how portfolio performance changes between estimation and subsequent evaluation periods, and whether the relative behaviour of the models is consistent across investment origins and market regimes. Three questions guide the analysis. First, to what extent do favourable in-sample dominance and performance measures persist out of sample? Second, how do risk, wealth accumulation and model feasibility vary with the market conditions prevailing at the investment origin? Third, what incremental performance is associated with deploying later stochastic decisions using the information available at the time, compared with retaining the fitted initial allocation?

To address these questions, the study uses rolling investment origins separated by one week. Each model is refitted on the preceding six calendar months and evaluated over the following calendar month. A further origin-specific evaluation follows the remaining holdings and cash through the available history after that month. The comparison jointly considers netSD, mean return, volatility, Sharpe and Sortino ratios, the Rachev ratio, drawdown, conditional value-at-risk and accumulated wealth. Transaction costs, turnover and constraint feasibility provide additional evidence on the practical implications of the allocations.

The contribution is a common empirical framework linking portfolio optimisation, information-adapted deployment and subsequent performance across repeated chronological evaluations. Interpretation is conditional on the study’s frozen investment universe, inventory and original constraint specification. Weekly origins produce overlapping evaluation periods, so the comparisons are descriptive and dependent; their results establish the behaviour of the specified historical experiment and provide a basis for further prospective validation.

3.5.2. Literature review

3.5.2.1. Portfolio theory and the distinction between optimisation and evaluation

Modern portfolio theory treats investment risk as a property of the joint distribution of asset returns. In Markowitz’s formulation, diversification depends on covariance as well as individual volatility, and efficient allocations balance expected return against portfolio variance. A security’s contribution therefore depends on the other holdings and their weights. The efficient frontier describes the opportunities implied by the assumed inputs and constraints; its interpretation depends on how those inputs were obtained. An allocation can be efficient under an estimated distribution while delivering disappointing subsequent results. Markowitz (1952) thus provides the allocation framework, while empirical validation addresses the reliability of its application.

Expected returns and covariance matrices must be estimated. Optimisation can amplify small estimation errors by assigning large weights to assets that appear unusually attractive in the available sample. DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal compare fourteen allocation models across seven datasets and find that none consistently dominates equal weighting on their reported out-of-sample criteria. This is evidence about the difficulty of overcoming estimation risk, rather than a universal prescription for equal weights. Jagannathan and Ma show that nonnegative weight constraints can reduce estimation-induced risk. These findings motivate simple benchmarks and constrained alternatives, whose advantages still require subsequent evaluation. DeMiguel et al. (2009); Jagannathan and Ma (2003).

3.5.2.2. What full-history performance can establish

Full-history analysis remains valuable for describing realised returns, drawdowns, correlations, concentration and exposure to particular episodes. A fixed-composition historical chart answers how a specified basket would have behaved over observed prices, subject to its accounting assumptions. An optimisation using all available observations identifies an allocation favoured by that fitted empirical distribution. These are legitimate descriptive questions. They differ from evaluating a decision rule that had to select investments before the subsequent returns became available. In this report, full-period diagnostics and chronological validation are consequently complementary sources of evidence. A backtest can cover the full available span and still include valid out-of-sample evaluations when each decision is reconstructed from prior information and the evaluation design is fixed in advance. The decisive issue is which observations informed each decision and model choice.

The distinction becomes concrete when a portfolio is selected using returns from 2021–2026 and then evaluated on those same years. Its weights incorporate information from the entire interval, including events occurring after the proposed investment date. Changing the objective from variance to netSD or tail risk leaves this information problem in place. Even without an explicit optimiser, selecting assets, technical thresholds or scenario settings after inspecting their historical outcomes can favour chance patterns. White’s analysis of data snooping explains why reusing a dataset for specification search changes the interpretation of the best observed result. A longer reused sample does not create an independent evaluation period. White (2000).

3.5.2.3. In-sample, out-of-sample and rolling-origin designs

In-sample evaluation measures behaviour on observations available during estimation. It is useful for diagnosing fitted relationships, numerical feasibility and model sensitivity. Out-of-sample evaluation applies a fitted specification to subsequent observations excluded from that fit. The separation must include the complete estimation pipeline: transformations, forecasting parameters, regime models, clustering and allocation decisions. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos describe rolling-origin evaluation as repeated forecasting from successive information cutoffs. This establishes the relevant chronology: a forecast is judged against observations occurring after its own training period. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos (2021, Chapter 5).

A single chronological split can be strongly influenced by its chosen market episode. Repeated origins reveal whether performance changes with the investment date. Cerqueira, Torgo and Mozetič compare validation procedures across real and synthetic time series, finding that their relative reliability depends on the data-generating setting; repeated holdouts perform particularly well in their non-stationary experiments. Their results support examining several temporally ordered tests, while providing no universal justification for this report’s exact window lengths. The present six-month estimation window, one-month test and weekly shift are declared experimental choices. Their robustness would require additional, separately specified comparisons. Cerqueira et al. (2020).

Rolling and expanding estimation windows also answer different questions. A rolling window drops older observations, allowing adaptation while retaining fewer data for estimation. An expanding window retains the growing history, potentially stabilising estimates but increasing exposure to older conditions. There is no automatic guarantee that either choice performs best for every asset or objective. For the present design, six months supply relatively recent information, but can contain few observations of a stressed regime. A one-month test offers repeated feedback while producing noisy tail estimates. These are design trade-offs to be examined in Results, rather than conclusions established by the literature.

3.5.2.4. Temporal dependence, overlapping tests and information leakage

Time-series validation requires attention to dependence and the information set. Randomly distributing observations across training and test sets can admit later information into an earlier decision. However, the statement that ordinary cross-validation is always invalid for time series is too broad. Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo establish conditions under which standard cross-validation is valid for autoregressive prediction with uncorrelated errors. Those conditions must be assessed for the intended model and task. Here, chronological evaluation is directly aligned with the decision problem, including published-price lags and sequential trading. The methodological argument concerns this deployment setting, rather than a blanket prohibition of every alternative validation method. Bergmeir et al. (2018).

Weekly origins with one-month tests produce overlapping evaluation periods. Adjacent portfolios experience many of the same market days, so their outcomes are dependent. The general econometric problem of sampling more frequently than the forecast horizon is central to Hansen and Hodrick’s work on multi-period forecasts. Applied here, overlap means that the number of origins is not the number of independent market experiments. It also prevents multiplying successive overlapping monthly returns into one investor’s wealth path. A continuous strategy would require a separate specification of how simultaneous allocations share capital. Paired, dependence-aware inference would be needed before interpreting a difference in average performance as statistically established. Hansen and Hodrick (1980).

3.5.2.5. Model selection, survivorship and the limits of a holdout

An evaluation period can cease to function as an independent test when its results repeatedly guide model redesign. Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu examine this problem through the probability of backtest overfitting and propose combinatorially symmetric cross-validation as a diagnostic. Their analysis highlights how selecting the most attractive result from many configurations can produce misleading expectations. For this report, comparing several optimisers, sale policies, scenario structures and performance measures creates a model-selection issue even when each individual fit respects chronology. A final claim about a selected winner would require an additional evaluation whose outcomes did not influence that selection. The probability of backtest overfitting itself has not been estimated here. Bailey et al. (2017).

The composition of the historical dataset matters as well. Brown, Goetzmann, Ibbotson and Ross demonstrate that survivorship selection can generate apparent persistence in performance. A study restricted to instruments available today may omit investments that disappeared or became ineligible. In the present experiment, the universe and initial inventory are retrospective fixed assumptions, so chronological refitting cannot establish that the same opportunity set was available to a historical investor. Similarly, origin-specific post-OOS tails have different lengths, and a later origin may train on observations that were held out at an earlier origin. These tails extend each origin’s assessment, while the overall design retains no globally untouched final sample. Brown et al. (1992).

3.5.2.6. Performance criteria, trading costs and sequential decisions

Portfolio quality is multidimensional. Mean return and accumulated wealth describe growth; volatility and drawdown describe different aspects of risk. The Sharpe ratio summarises reward relative to variability, but its estimation and temporal aggregation depend on return properties. Lo shows that serial correlation can materially affect Sharpe-ratio inference and annualisation. Downside and tail criteria add further information: Rockafellar and Uryasev develop portfolio optimisation based on conditional value-at-risk. Their work motivates examining losses in adverse outcomes alongside average performance. In a one-month sample, however, a five-percent tail may be represented by roughly one observation, making tail comparisons particularly sensitive to individual days. Lo (2002); Rockafellar and Uryasev (2000).

The link to implementation also requires explicit accounting. Turnover, transaction costs, cash and binding constraints can change the outcome of an otherwise attractive allocation. This report therefore evaluates returns together with trading costs and feasibility. Its netSD measure additionally depends on the comparison network and candidate-specific denominator; agreement between a scenario-law objective and a realised historical score cannot be presumed. For stochastic portfolios, the initial decision and the subsequent policy are separate objects of evaluation. Later trades must use information available at their execution date. Reporting initial-only allocations and observed-prefix deployments separately makes the contribution of later decisions assessable within the stated historical experiment.

3.5.2.7. Implications for the present empirical study

Taken together, the literature motivates chronological refitting, transparent benchmarks, explicit investment constraints and evaluation across several performance dimensions. Full-history analysis provides context and diagnostics; rolling tests assess subsequent behaviour under repeated information cutoffs. The present study combines these roles while retaining the frozen research inventory and original model contract. Results should be interpreted through sample coverage, regime conditions, costs and deployment rules, with mathematically infeasible cases disclosed separately. A consistently favourable descriptive comparison would justify further investigation and prospective testing. The strength of any claim about generalisable investment performance depends on an additional evaluation protected from the choices made during this research.

3.5.3. Methodology — windows, information and deployment

Origins move seven calendar days, mapped backwards to an observed session. IS is (origin minus six calendar months, origin]; OOS is (origin, origin plus one calendar month]; the per-origin held-out tail starts after OOS and ends at the dataset endpoint. Only complete one-month tests enter. Later origins may train on earlier held-out observations: there is no globally untouched final set.

\[\mathcal I_k=(t_k-6\,\mathrm{months},t_k],\quad \mathcal O_k=(t_k,t_k+1\,\mathrm{month}],\quad \mathcal H_k=(\max\mathcal O_k,T]\]

Each fit sees only its physical training files. US information lags one observed session, NAV information two; no future prices bridge stale marks. The universe, current inventory and sector exposures are retrospective fixed design assumptions. Every fitted moment, HMM, residual model, cluster and policy is estimated again inside that window.

Current; Markowitz, Minimax, Robust, Update2, Update3, netSD and regime-netSD Sale/No-sale; Stochastic, stochA, Stochastic2 and stoch2A with all three sale policies are covered. C4 is 20×20×10×4. Native initial allocations and approximate causal observed-prefix deployments are separate. Native two-stage oracle recourse cannot be executed causally unchanged. After the final within-month decision, remaining quantities and cash are frozen through the held-out tail.

Six-month study settings differ explicitly from long-history production eligibility: at least 20 weekly prices and 16 joint weekly residuals (daily residual minimum 40). HMMs retain native six-start two-state fits but omit an unnecessary inner80% split. Failed ensemble members and unavailable regime-specific fits are reported, never fabricated. If both World fits fail the20-observation effective state-mass floor, only the intercept-only model is retried with30 starts and a six-observation floor; this explicitly marked short-window recovery has weak tail-state evidence. At least three valid Baltic indices and a valid World model are required. Surviving members receive equal within-index weights, with equal index weights. Forward-only future regime probabilities use frozen window parameters; smoothed future labels never inform trading.

Standard EUR1,000 total/EUR750 per-asset gross purchase caps, A removal of both caps, 30% sector constraint, physical lots, cash and future-US cumulative purchase limits are retained. Next-observed-valuation paper execution includes 0/10/25/50bps costs (primary10bps). This is not guaranteed broker execution or a historical NAV publication archive. Valuation uses price marks and zero-interest model cash; cash dividend distributions and tax are excluded. The dividend-derived penalty uses available completed prior-year inputs; unavailable prior-year features take a conservative zero assumption, not a claim that the issuer paid no dividend. Marked sector drift and rejected purchases are recorded.

3.5.4. Methodology — performance, netSD and uncertainty
\[V_t=c_t+\sum_iq_{i,t}P_{i,t},\quad r_t=V_t/V_{t-1}-1,\quad \bar r=\frac1n\sum_t r_t,\quad s_r^2=\frac{\sum_t(r_t-\bar r)^2}{n-1}\]
\[SR=\bar r/s_r,\quad SoR=\bar r/\sqrt{n^{-1}\sum_t\min(r_t,0)^2},\quad MDD=\max_t\left(1-\frac{V_t}{\max_{u\le t}V_u}\right)\]

Sharpe and Sortino use zero risk-free/target returns; annualised ratios multiply by √252. Rachev divides the upper5% fractional tail mean by minus the lower5% mean; CVaR95 is minus that lower mean. A monthly5% tail is often approximately one observation. Undefined ratios remain unavailable. IS curves are fitted origin weights rebased at the training start, not historical executed trades.

\[\operatorname{netSD}(p)=\frac{d_p^{out}-d_p^{in}}{\max_{v\in G\cup\{p\}}(d_v^{out}+d_v^{in})}\]

Every daily empirical netSD uses the matched116-asset reference network plus Current and a candidate-dependent augmented denominator. These IS/OOS/held-out daily-network values differ from the native four-week terminal-law objective, which is exported separately. No component-weighted netSD shortcut is used.

Overlapping one-month windows are dependent. Their monthly returns are never compounded into a fictitious continuous account. The accumulated wealth chart is one selected origin's self-financing trajectory. The held-out periods have different lengths. Comparisons are descriptive; any confidence intervals must use paired dependence-aware resampling, and model selection requires an additional untouched test. No IID p-values or purported winner are supplied.

3.5.7. Conclusions and limitations

No conclusion is drawn from unfinished windows. Short estimation samples, stale marks, retrospective universe/inventory, heuristic optimisation, approximate out-of-tree mapping and small monthly tail samples limit interpretation. Performance is not a guarantee of future returns.
